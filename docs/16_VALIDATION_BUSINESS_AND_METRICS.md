# Validation, adoption, business and impact

## 1. Research questions

Does reviewed evidence change what recruiters can understand about a candidate? Is the review effort acceptable? Does a specific challenge help a beginner take action? Can a college coordinator use the gap report to choose an intervention? Will stakeholders use a shared system instead of creating additional spreadsheets? These questions should guide product iteration.

## 2. Interview guide

Ask students to describe their last internship application, what evidence they included, what confused them and what feedback they received. Ask them to show the workflow if comfortable. Do not lead with “would you use our AI portal?” Ask whether a short task feels helpful or like unpaid work, and what time commitment is reasonable.

Ask employers how they currently shortlist, what evidence they trust, how long screening takes and who could review a challenge. Show one normal profile and one evidence-based profile and ask them to explain their decision. Ask what information they would never upload and how they handle candidate complaints.

Ask faculty/placement staff which reports they maintain, where data becomes stale and what actual training decision they made recently. Show the gap table and ask what they would do next. If they cannot name an action, simplify or reconsider the report.

Target five students, three employers and two faculty/coordinators for initial discovery. This is a proposed qualitative sample, not statistically representative research. Record consent and anonymize notes. Do not claim these interviews have occurred.

## 3. Pilot design

Propose a four-week pilot with one department, roughly 20 consenting students, two participating employers and three scoped challenges. These numbers are planning assumptions. Week 1: baseline workflow and rubric calibration. Week 2: challenge participation. Week 3: review, feedback and applications. Week 4: interviews with users and workflow evaluation. Employment outcomes need longer follow-up.

Have reviewers assess a few shared samples before live submissions and record disagreements. Limit the number of submissions per reviewer. If queues grow, pause challenge intake or add approved reviewers rather than promising immediate review.

## 4. Measurement dictionary

| Metric | Definition | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence activation | Students with ≥1 published review / active participants | Core adoption |
| Evidence-to-application use | Students applying with reviewed evidence / active participants | North-star pilot measure |
| Review turnaround | Median hours from finalization to publication | Service burden |
| Review effort | Median reviewer minutes per submission | Scalability constraint |
| Applicant-to-interview | Interviewed applicants / submitted applications | Funnel, not causal uplift |
| Confirmed completion | Confirmed completed internships / accepted internships due to finish | Outcome integrity |
| Gap action rate | Coordinators recording an intervention / report viewers | P1 actionability |
| Correction rate | Correction requests / published reviews | Diagnostic, not automatically failure |

Deduplicate by the relevant student/application ID. State observation windows and exclude not-yet-due internships from completion denominators. Track qualitative complaints and withdrawals alongside positive metrics.

## 5. Proposed pilot decision thresholds

Discussion targets: at least half of participating students complete one reviewed artifact; median review effort below 15 minutes for the bounded challenge; most tested recruiters can explain the score and evidence without coaching; at least one coordinator identifies a concrete support action. These are proposed go/no-go criteria, not achieved outcomes. Revise them with stakeholders before the pilot.

Do not infer placement uplift from a small self-selected group. Compare screening time on equivalent tasks and record selection bias. A high completion rate with poor student experience is not success. A low correction rate may reflect difficulty reporting problems, so inspect the reporting process.

## 6. Adoption strategy

Start from an existing college–employer relationship. Offer a ready-to-edit challenge template and a short onboarding walkthrough. Keep the first pilot narrow: one role family, clear deadlines and named reviewers. Recruit students through approved college channels after consent. Do not scrape resumes or send outreach without authorization.

Avoid the marketplace cold-start trap by scheduling a cohort challenge with a known reviewer before opening public signups. The portal should support the collaboration already being organized; a network effect cannot substitute for initial relationships.

## 7. Business hypotheses

Keep student access free. Test an institutional subscription for workflow/reporting and optional employer-sponsored challenge programs. Do not charge for a stronger skill score or a favorable review. Avoid selling student data. Pricing requires discovery, procurement understanding and cost measurement; no revenue forecast is presented as established.

Costs include hosting, database, support and reviewer coordination. For a challenge program, contribution margin equals program revenue minus reviewer/support time and attributable infrastructure costs. Even if software hosting is inexpensive, human review can dominate cost. Investigate whether employers, institutions or both are willing to own that work.

## 8. Long-term defensibility

Potential strengths are trusted reviewer relationships, reusable high-quality rubrics, institutional workflow fit and consented longitudinal evidence. A large AI prompt or a generic score is easy to copy. Portability and transparent evaluation can improve trust, but must be tested rather than treated as automatic competitive advantages.

## 9. Social impact and unintended effects

Potential benefits include a clearer route for students without polished resumes and better recognition of practical projects. Potential harms include extra unpaid work, reviewer favoritism, overreliance on numbers and exclusion of students with limited connectivity. Mitigate with bounded tasks, transparent criteria, alternative evidence formats, human decisions and accessible low-bandwidth screens. Measure who drops out and why before claiming inclusion.
